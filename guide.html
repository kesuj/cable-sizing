The idea in two minutes

A cable is a pipe for current. Too thin and it gets hot, wastes power and, in a fault, melts before the breaker trips. Too thick and you pay for copper you never use. Sizing finds the thinnest cable that passes four tests.

It must not overheatEvery cable has a current it can carry all day without its insulation cooking. Heat around it — summer sun, soil, other cables — lowers that number.
The voltage must arriveLong thin cables lose voltage on the way. Lights dim, motors run hot and fail to start. Nepal's law allows the supply only ±5 %.
It must survive a short circuitFor the split second before the breaker trips, thousands of amps flow. The cable must be thick enough not to be damaged.
The breaker must protect itThe breaker trips before the cable overheats: load current ≤ breaker rating ≤ what the cable can carry.

The calculator runs all four tests on every standard size, picks the smallest that passes, and tells you which test decided it. Often it is not the one you expect: on long runs it is voltage drop, near big transformers it is the short-circuit test.

The complete process

These ten steps are the same in every country; only the tables and limits change. Formulas, clauses and worked examples are in docs/METHOD.md and docs/WORKED_EXAMPLES.md.

  1. Load current. 3-phase: I = P ÷ (√3 × 400 V × pf × η). Single phase: I = P ÷ (230 V × pf × η). 1 HP = 0.746 kW of shaft power.
  2. Design current Ib. Add spare for growth (25 % for a building supply) and 25 % for a motor branch.
  3. Breaker or fuse. The next standard rating at or above Ib: 6, 10, 16, 20, 25, 32, 40, 63, 80, 100, 125 A …
  4. Rating factors. Multiply the factors for air or ground temperature, soil, depth and neighbouring cables.
  5. Thermal size. The table rating must be at least breaker rating ÷ total factor.
  6. Voltage drop. ΔU = √3 × I × L × (R cos φ + X sin φ). Keep it within 3–5 %.
  7. Motor starting. A direct-on-line motor draws 6–7 times its current while starting; the dip must not stall it.
  8. Short circuit. Cross-section ≥ I × √t ÷ k (k = 143 copper XLPE, 94 aluminium XLPE, 115/76 for PVC).
  9. Parallel cables. If no single cable is enough, use two or more identical cables side by side (they heat each other, so each is derated).
  10. Earth conductor. Same size up to 16 mm², 16 mm² up to 35 mm², half the phase size above that.
The two rules that catch most mistakes

Size the cable for the breaker, not for the load. If a 20 A load is on a 32 A breaker, the cable must carry 32 A after derating, or it can overheat for hours without the breaker ever tripping.

Never mix tables. Indian (IS) ratings assume 40 °C air and 30 °C ground; IEC ratings assume 30 °C air and 20 °C ground. Using IEC factors on IS ratings (or the reverse) gives wrong answers of 10–20 %.

Where the cable runs changes everything

The same 16 mm² copper cable can safely carry anywhere from about 60 A to 100 A depending on how it is installed, because what limits it is how fast heat escapes.

How it is done in Nepal

There is no separate Nepal wiring code. The rules come from the Electricity Regulation 2050 and the National Building Code NBC 207, and NBC 207 points to Indian Standards for the actual numbers. In practice, IS tables from Indian and Nepali manufacturers are the national default. Projects funded by ADB, AIIB or JICA usually follow IEC, so the calculator offers both.

What NEA networks look like

  • LV 230/400 V from 50, 100 and 200 kVA distribution transformers; aerial bundled cable 25–150 mm² from Nepali makers (IS 14255).
  • 11 kV overhead: ACSR Dog (100 mm²), Rabbit (50 mm²), Weasel (30 mm²). 33 kV rural lines also use Dog.
  • 11 kV underground: 3-core 300 mm² aluminium XLPE armoured has appeared in NEA tenders (rated 28.2 kA for 1 s).
  • 132 kV: ACSR Bear; 220 and 400 kV: ACSR Moose; some 132 kV lines re-conductored with high-temperature ACCC.
  • NEA's design criteria for distribution projects keep feeder voltage drop under 5 % at the target-year load.

What you must find out yourself

These are not published; ask NEA's distribution centre or your consultant: the fault level at your point of supply (11 kV switchgear in Kathmandu is rated 25 kA), the protection clearing time, and whether the LV network at your site is treated as TT or TN-C-S. With TT (local earth electrode only), an RCD is what disconnects an earth fault fast — fit 30 mA on socket circuits.

Altitude: no cable standard applies an altitude factor; the cooler air at height already raises the rating. Altitude derating above ~1000 m is for equipment — breakers, drives, inverters. Strong sun at altitude is the real risk: cables in direct sun are not covered by any table, so shade them or use a conduit.

High voltage: 3.3 to 33 kV

The steps are the same, with three differences.

  • There is no breaker rating to size for. The cable is sized on the load current, and the protection relay is then set at or below the cable's derated rating.
  • The short-circuit test usually decides. 13.1 kA for 1 second needs 139 mm² of aluminium whatever the load is. Ask NEA for the fault level and the clearing time.
  • The reference depth is deeper (0.9 m up to 11 kV, 1.05 m for 22–33 kV), and soil and temperature factors differ from LV. The calculator uses the MV tables from the Polycab HT catalogue, with KEI as a cross-check.

Earthed (E) grades suit systems where an earth fault is cleared in about a minute; unearthed (UE) grades are for systems that may run with an earth fault for longer. Single-core cables must have aluminium (non-magnetic) armour. The copper screen must carry the earth-fault current; check it in the datasheet.

Rating methods engineers use beyond catalogue tables
  • IEC 60287: the analytical method behind every table. Use it for unusual installations: deep burial, crossings, sun.
  • IEC 60949: short-circuit rating including heat lost to the insulation. Gives higher allowable currents for screens and small conductors than the simple adiabatic formula.
  • IEC 60502-2: tabulated MV ratings. Neher–McGrath / IEEE 835 / NEC 311: the US equivalents.
  • In Nepal, utility studies use ETAP for load flow and short circuit (IOE Pulchowk theses, OCEM journal).

Buying and installing

At the shop

  • The printing on the sheath must show the maker, the standard (IS 694, IS 1554, IS 7098), the size in mm², the number of cores and the voltage grade (1.1 kV, or 450/750 V for house wire).
  • Look for the ISI/NS mark. Avoid unmarked cable and "copper-clad aluminium" sold as copper — scrape a strand: it must be copper all the way through.
  • Count strands and check the weight on the label against the catalogue; undersized "2.5 mm²" wire is common.
  • Buy about 10 % more than the measured route length.

Laying it

  • Underground LV: at least 0.75 m deep (0.9 m for 11 kV, 1.05 m for 33 kV), on 75–100 mm of sand, covered with sand, then bricks or a cover slab, then warning tape 300 mm above.
  • Use pipe under roads, driveways and walls. Seal the pipe ends.
  • Do not bend tighter than 12 × the cable diameter (multicore) or 15–20 × for HV.
  • Terminate with glands and crimped lugs of the right size and material; use bimetallic lugs between aluminium cable and copper terminals.

Before switching on

  • Insulation test with a 500 V (LV) or 1000 V tester: phase-to-phase and phase-to-earth, at least 1 MΩ; new cable is normally hundreds of MΩ.
  • Check continuity of the earth conductor and the polarity at the far end.
  • Test the RCD with its button and, if possible, a tester. Measure the voltage at the far end under load and compare with the calculator.

Limits and sources

The calculator covers continuous ratings, voltage drop, motor starting, short circuit, parallel runs and earth conductor size. It flags but does not calculate: earth-fault loop impedance and disconnection time, harmonic (third-harmonic) neutral loading, cables in direct sun, and HV screen earth-fault capacity.
